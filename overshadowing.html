Diagnostic Overshadowing: The Standing Memo

When a hospital treats the psychiatric label instead of the injury, that is not just bad medicine — it can be disability discrimination with enforceable law behind it. Here is the scaffold: the statutes, the regulation, the case, and the accreditor's own warning. The record is the arbiter.

Definition Diagnostic overshadowing — the documented clinical failure pattern in which a patient's physical symptoms are attributed to a psychiatric or cognitive condition rather than evaluated on their own terms, causing physical illness and injury to go undertreated. First named in the disability literature in the 1980s; formally flagged as a patient-safety risk by The Joint Commission, the body that accredits American hospitals.

The Mandate — What Makes It Unlawful

Overshadowing becomes more than malpractice when a patient is treated differently because of a disability label. Four bodies of law reach that conduct:

Section 504 — Rehabilitation Act
29 U.S.C. § 794

Prohibits disability discrimination in any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance. Hospitals that bill Medicare or Medicaid — which is virtually all of them — are covered.

Section 1557 — Affordable Care Act
42 U.S.C. § 18116

The modern healthcare-specific nondiscrimination statute: incorporates Section 504's disability protections and applies them to health programs and activities receiving federal funds, with a private right of action.

Michigan Persons with Disabilities Civil Rights Act
MCL 37.1101 et seq.

Michigan's state-law prohibition on disability discrimination in public accommodations and services, including health facilities. Enforced through the Michigan Department of Civil Rights complaint process.

Restraint — Medicare Conditions of Participation
42 C.F.R. § 482.13(e)

Federal regulation binding on participating hospitals: restraint may be imposed only to ensure the immediate physical safety of the patient, staff, or others — and never for coercion, discipline, convenience, or retaliation. Michigan's Mental Health Code imposes parallel restraint and consent requirements, with recipient-rights enforcement.

The Standing — Who Has Recognized It

Bragdon v. Abbott
524 U.S. 624 (1998)

The Supreme Court held that disability discrimination law reaches healthcare itself: providers cannot treat patients differently because of their condition. Discrimination inside the exam room is still discrimination.

The Joint Commission — Patient Safety Advisory
Quick Safety on Diagnostic Overshadowing

The hospital accreditor itself has warned member hospitals that attributing physical symptoms to a psychiatric or cognitive label causes missed diagnoses and patient harm — meaning the failure mode is one hospitals are already on formal notice to prevent.

The Standard — The Honest Line

Courts distinguish malpractice from discrimination: a mere misdiagnosis or bad treatment decision is negligence, not a civil rights violation. The discrimination claim requires showing differential treatment because of the disability label — that a patient without the label, presenting with the same physical injury, would have received evaluation and treatment rather than sedation and restraint. Repeat presentations for the same untreated injury are what carry that comparison: they show a pattern, not a one-time judgment call. The claim is not "they treated my injury badly." The claim is "they treated me differently — and their own records show it."

A Live Fact Pattern — Michigan, 2026

The publisher of this site — an SSA-adjudicated disabled adult and the pro se plaintiff in McKendry v. PNC Bank, N.A., et al. (W.D. Mich.) — reports the following account, presented here as his allegations, currently the subject of pending administrative complaints and investigation:

The Injury

A foot injury requiring more than one emergency department visit at the same Michigan hospital in 2026, observed by multiple physicians and nurses across encounters.

The Overshadowing

He alleges that expressions of distress about the untreated injury were attributed to mental illness, and were answered with involuntary psychiatric admission, physical restraint, and involuntary administration of Ativan — rather than treatment of the injury.

The Record Being Built

Contemporaneous, dated, handwritten documentation made inside the unit; recipient-rights complaints filed from inside the ward, now under statutory 90-day investigation; a comprehensive medical-records request served under the HIPAA right of access; and administrative civil-rights complaints prepared for the Michigan Department of Civil Rights and the U.S. Department of Justice.
